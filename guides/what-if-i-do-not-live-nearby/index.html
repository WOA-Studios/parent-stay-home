What If I Do Not Live Nearby?

Helping from another city or state can feel like trying to protect someone with your arms stretched across miles. This guide helps you build a local support net, organize information, and create a just-in-case plan before you urgently need one.

Open long-distance checklist Find local aging resources

Start here

You do not have to be nearby to be useful.

Distance changes the kind of help you can provide, but it does not make your help less important. Your job is not to personally do every task. Your job may be to organize the information, build the local network, notice changes, ask questions, coordinate services, and know who can physically get there when you cannot.

  • Identify at least one trusted person who lives near your parent.
  • Know who has a key, code, building access, or another safe way in.
  • Write down doctors, pharmacy, hospital, medications, and insurance information.
  • Ask medical offices how they document permission to communicate with family or helpers.
  • Create a plan for what to do if your parent suddenly stops answering.
Urgent safety note: If you believe your parent may be in immediate danger, may have fallen, may be having a medical emergency, may be confused or wandering, may be unable to reach the phone, or the situation feels urgent, call 911 or the appropriate local emergency service right away.

One helpful question

Who can physically get there if I cannot?

Write down a first nearby contact, a backup contact, and anyone who has safe access to the home. Do this before you need them.

Build the local net

Create a small team near your parent.

A dependable local network may matter more than having one person trying to do everything. Keep the list realistic and ask each person what they are actually comfortable doing.

Nearby trusted person

A neighbor, friend, relative, faith-community member, building manager, or other trusted person who can check in when something seems wrong.

Backup nearby contact

Do not rely on one person. Your first contact may be traveling, working, ill, or simply unreachable when you need them.

Home access

Know who has a spare key, lockbox code, building access, or other authorized way to enter. Avoid putting access codes or passwords into places that are not secure.

Medical communication

Do not wait for a crisis to learn who can receive information.

Being an adult child does not automatically mean every doctor, hospital, pharmacy, or health plan will discuss everything with you. Ask your parent and each office how permission is documented and what they need on file.

  • Ask your parent whom they want involved in medical communication.
  • Ask each doctor or facility how they document permission to speak with family or helpers.
  • Record whether an authorization, release, portal permission, or other office form is on file.
  • Confirm the names and phone numbers of the people your parent wants involved.
  • Recheck permissions after changes in doctors, hospitals, insurance, or caregiving arrangements.

Use the medical communication access tracker

HIPAA note: HIPAA can allow healthcare providers to share information that is directly relevant to a family member, friend, or other person involved in a patient’s care when the patient agrees or does not object. In some situations when the patient is unavailable or incapacitated, a provider may share relevant information if, using professional judgment, it is in the patient’s best interest. Providers are not always required to share, and office procedures may differ. Ask the specific provider or health plan what documentation they use.

If I cannot reach my parent

Make the just-in-case plan before panic makes it for you.

You cannot predict every emergency. You can decide the first few steps ahead of time.

Know the normal pattern

Write down when your parent usually answers calls, texts, or checks in. A missed call at an unusual time may mean something different from a missed call during a regular nap, appointment, or activity.

Decide the first steps

For example: call again, send a text, try the house phone, check known appointments, contact the first nearby person, then contact the backup person.

Keep local numbers ready

Record the local police non-emergency number, local fire/EMS non-emergency number if available, building management or front desk, and community office when applicable.

Know when to escalate

If you have reason to believe your parent may be in immediate danger, may have fallen, may be having a medical emergency, may be unable to get to the phone, or the situation feels urgent, call 911 or the appropriate local emergency service.

Safety reminder: Do not ask a neighbor, friend, family member, or helper to enter a situation that may be unsafe. Emergency responders may be the appropriate first call when danger is possible.

What to organize

Keep the information you may need during one unexpected phone call.

Medical

  • Primary doctor and specialists
  • Current medications and allergies
  • Pharmacy
  • Preferred hospital
  • Insurance information

Home

  • Street address and apartment/unit
  • Key or safe-access plan
  • Trusted nearby contacts
  • Pets or household needs
  • Known home safety concerns

Practical support

  • Transportation
  • Meals and groceries
  • Medication pickup or delivery
  • Regular appointments
  • Who usually checks in

Important papers

  • Where key documents are kept
  • Who can access them
  • Emergency information sheet
  • Relevant legal/healthcare documents
  • Trusted professional contacts

Watch for change

Distance can hide small warning signs.

A parent may sound “fine” on the phone while meals are being skipped, mail is piling up, medications are confused, the home is becoming unsafe, or transportation is failing. Look for patterns instead of trying to diagnose from a distance.

  • Missed calls or unusual difficulty reaching your parent
  • Repeated missed appointments
  • Food, hydration, or grocery concerns
  • Medication mix-ups
  • New falls, bruises, or mobility problems
  • Unpaid bills, unusual spending, or possible exploitation
  • Home maintenance or safety concerns
  • Isolation or sudden withdrawal from usual routines

One next step

Build the first layer of your local backup plan.

Today, identify one nearby person your parent trusts, confirm how to reach them, and ask whether they are willing to be a just-in-case contact. Then add a backup person. You do not need the entire plan finished to make the next emergency less chaotic.
