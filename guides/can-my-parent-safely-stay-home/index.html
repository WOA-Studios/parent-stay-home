Can My Parent Safely Stay Home?

A calm starting point for thinking through safety, support, and next steps without shame or panic.

Open home safety checklist Find home safety help

Start here

Safety is not about perfection.

Many parents can remain at home with the right support, changes, and check-ins. Others may need more help than the home can safely provide right now. The goal is not to force one answer. The goal is to notice what is happening, reduce risk where possible, and know when to ask for qualified help.

  • Start with the concern that feels most urgent.
  • Write down what changed, when it started, and how often it happens.
  • Look for patterns, not just one isolated moment.
  • Include your parent in the conversation whenever possible and safe.
  • Ask the right professional, agency, or emergency resource when safety is unclear.
Urgent safety note: If your parent may be in immediate danger, may have fallen, may be having a medical emergency, may be unable to get to the phone, or the situation feels urgent, call 911 or the appropriate local emergency service right away.

What to look at

Seven areas that can affect staying home safely.

You do not have to answer every question today. Use these areas to notice what may need attention first.

1. Falls, walking, and getting around

Notice falls, near-falls, trouble with stairs, difficulty getting in and out of chairs, uneven walkways, bathroom risk, or new fear of moving around the home.

2. Food, meals, and hydration

Look for spoiled food, skipped meals, weight changes, difficulty shopping or cooking, dehydration concerns, or trouble opening packages and preparing food safely.

3. Medications and medical follow-up

Watch for missed doses, duplicate doses, expired medications, confusion about instructions, missed appointments, or trouble reaching doctors, pharmacies, or specialists.

4. Memory, judgment, and wandering risk

Notice getting lost, leaving appliances on, confusion about time or place, repeated calls about the same issue, unsafe decisions, or leaving the home unexpectedly.

5. Hygiene, laundry, and household upkeep

Pay attention to bathing, clothing, laundry, trash, pests, clutter, blocked pathways, mail piling up, or changes in how the home is being maintained.

6. Bills, money, and exploitation concerns

Look for unpaid bills, shutoff notices, unusual withdrawals, new “helpers” pressuring your parent, missing money, scams, or confusion about accounts and benefits.

7. Emergency response and caregiver capacity

Consider whether your parent can call for help, unlock the door, explain what happened, reach a phone, use a medical alert device, and whether the current caregiver support is enough.

One helpful question

What could happen if nothing changes? The answer can help you decide whether the next step is a checklist, a phone call, a home safety change, a service, or urgent help.

Possible next supports

Help at home may reduce some risks.

A concern does not always mean your parent has to leave home. Sometimes the next step is adding the right support.

Home safety changes

Grab bars, ramps, bathroom safety, lighting, stair support, accessibility updates, and home modifications may make daily life safer.

Meal delivery and food assistance

Meals, nutrition programs, and food support may help when shopping or cooking becomes difficult.

Senior transportation

Transportation may help with appointments, errands, groceries, senior programs, and reducing isolation.

Local aging resources

County, state, and local aging organizations may help families understand available services and next steps.

Long-distance caregiving plan

If you do not live nearby, create a just-in-case plan with local contacts, access information, and medical communication notes.

Emergency information sheet

Gather contacts, doctors, medications, allergies, pharmacy, insurance, and access details before they are urgently needed.

Conversation help

Talk about support without making your parent feel like a burden.

Many parents fear losing control, privacy, independence, or dignity. It may help to frame support as a way to protect the life they still want, not as punishment for needing help.

  • “I want to understand what feels harder lately.”
  • “What would make home feel easier or safer?”
  • “Can we try one small support first and see how it feels?”
  • “I am not trying to take over. I want us to have a plan.”
  • “Who would you trust to help if I cannot get there quickly?”

Before you call for help

Write down the concern, the location in the home, how often it happens, what has already been tried, and who has noticed it. Clear details can reduce back-and-forth when speaking with providers, agencies, or professionals.

When staying home may need more support

It may be time to ask for more help if safety concerns keep repeating.

Parent Stay Home supports helping a parent remain at home for as long as reasonably possible — not at all costs. If your parent is repeatedly falling, missing important medications, wandering, unable to get food, being exploited, unable to respond in emergencies, living with serious untreated hazards, or if caregiver support is no longer enough, speak with a qualified professional, local aging agency, healthcare provider, or emergency resource about additional options.

General information note: This guide is for organization and planning only. It is not medical, legal, financial, or care-management advice. For decisions about healthcare, legal documents, benefits, taxes, accounts, medications, home safety, or urgent concerns, contact the appropriate qualified professional, agency, or emergency service.

One next step

You do not have to decide everything today.

Choose the concern that feels most urgent. Write down what changed. Use one checklist. Make one call. The goal is not to have every answer — the goal is to make the next step less chaotic.
