Caregiving coordination guide

I'm the Only One Taking Care of My Aging Parent. Where Can I Get Help?

Sometimes one person becomes the family scheduler, driver, paperwork organizer, and emergency contact without anyone formally agreeing to that arrangement. If you're carrying most of the work, here is a practical place to start.

Start with understanding

The work is often bigger than anyone sees.

You may be making appointments, answering calls, managing transportation, tracking paperwork, checking the refrigerator, and arranging backup when plans change. Recognizing the whole workload helps you see which responsibilities could be shared, simplified, or supported by a service.

Feeling seen matters. Getting help matters, too. You do not need to solve everything in one conversation.

Start with the older adult. Ask which tasks they want help with, which they prefer to handle themselves, who they want included in planning or updates, and who they do not want involved. Respect their decisions and privacy where they can make those choices, subject to applicable legal requirements and safety needs.

Step 1

Write down what's actually being done.

Include the visible and invisible tasks. A simple list can make it easier to discuss support without blame.

Appointments and health logistics

Scheduling, rides, pharmacy pickup, preparing questions, and sharing information with authorized people.

Meals and daily activities

Groceries, food preparation, deliveries, laundry, and regular check-ins.

Home and safety

Repairs, seasonal upkeep, lighting, entry access, and checking hazards.

Paperwork and administration

Mail, insurance, benefit questions, essential records, taxes, and coordination with authorized professionals.

Step 2

Ask what the parent wants—and what each helper can realistically offer.

A nearby sibling might handle rides; a distant relative might arrange appointments or research services. A trusted friend may agree to check in. Contributions do not need to look identical, and no one should be listed as a helper until they agree.

  • Which tasks does the parent want help with?
  • Which people may be included—or excluded—from planning and updates?
  • Who can reliably accept each task and how often?
  • Does the helper have the necessary permission or legal authority?
  • Which tasks remain uncovered?

Not everyone has siblings or a cooperative family. If nobody can commit to a task, leave it unassigned and consider community, nonprofit, or paid support instead of assuming one person must absorb it.

Step 3

Agree on a backup plan before someone is unavailable.

For each recurring task, name the primary helper, a backup who has agreed to step in, and the schedule or trigger. Discuss what happens when work, illness, travel, family obligations, or an emergency make the usual arrangement impossible.

  1. Choose one way to request coverage (for example, a call or a group message with the parent's permission).
  2. Specify how much notice to give when possible.
  3. Agree who will arrange outside assistance when no family coverage is available.
  4. Set a date to review the arrangement, and revisit it when circumstances change.

A shared calendar can help with dates, but it cannot replace a clear agreement on who has accepted responsibility.

Step 5

Look beyond the family when needed.

Depending on where your parent lives, help may be available for transportation, meals, home modifications, respite, or care coordination. Start with your local Area Agency on Aging or other community aging resources. Ask about eligibility, costs, service area, scheduling, waitlists, and what assistance is actually included.

One next step

Start with one responsibility that needs reliable coverage.

Choose a task that repeatedly causes stress, ask the person receiving care what they prefer, and check whether a willing helper or service can take it on. Write down the agreed plan and a backup.

You deserve a plan that does not depend entirely on what one person can remember or carry.

Respect choices and legal boundaries.

Care coordination must respect the older adult's expressed wishes and privacy where they have decision-making capacity. A voluntary family agreement or worksheet does not create legal authority to make medical or financial decisions, control property, or exclude someone where valid law or orders provide otherwise.

Safety: If there is immediate danger or a medical emergency, call emergency services. If care needs or caregiver capacity exceed what is safe at home, seek professional assessment and discuss additional care options.
