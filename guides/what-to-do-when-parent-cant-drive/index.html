What to Do When Your Parent Can’t Drive

Losing driving can feel like losing freedom. This guide helps you think through safety, transportation, errands, appointments, and connection without shame or panic.

Start here

This is not just about keys.

Driving often represents independence, routine, privacy, and control. When driving changes, your parent may also lose easy access to groceries, doctors, pharmacy, friends, faith community, senior programs, and ordinary errands. The goal is not to argue first. The goal is to understand what driving currently provides and replace as much of that support as possible.

  • Start with the most urgent safety concern.
  • Write down what changed, when it happened, and who noticed it.
  • Think about what your parent uses driving for each week.
  • Look for transportation options before taking anything away, when possible.
  • Involve your parent in planning whenever it is safe and appropriate.
Urgent safety note: If your parent may be in immediate danger while driving, may be lost, may be confused, may have had an accident, or the situation feels urgent, call 911 or the appropriate local emergency service right away.

What changed?

Notice the concern before deciding the solution.

A driving concern may come from eyesight, medications, reaction time, memory changes, a recent fall, a medical event, anxiety, or a change in confidence. PSH does not diagnose or determine fitness to drive, but these signs can help you decide what to discuss with a qualified professional.

Possible warning signs

  • Recent accident, near miss, or new dents on the car.
  • Getting lost on familiar routes.
  • Confusion about traffic signs, lights, or lanes.
  • New fear, anger, or anxiety while driving.
  • Missed turns, delayed reactions, or trouble parking.
  • Medication changes, dizziness, vision changes, or medical episodes.

Write down specifics

Before calling a doctor, agency, or family meeting, write down the concern in plain language: what happened, when it happened, whether it has happened before, and what risk it created. Specific examples are more useful than “I just think Dad should stop driving.”

Replace the function

What does driving help your parent do?

Instead of starting with “you cannot drive,” start by naming the needs driving has been meeting. Then look for replacement supports.

Medical appointments

Look for senior transportation, paratransit, county rides, volunteer driver programs, caregiver rides, or non-emergency medical transportation if appropriate.

Groceries and meals

If driving was how your parent got food, consider grocery delivery, meal delivery, senior nutrition programs, pantry support, or family/helper grocery rotations.

Find meal and food support

Pharmacy and prescriptions

Ask the pharmacy about delivery, automatic refills, medication synchronization, pill packaging, or caregiver pickup authorization where appropriate.

Social connection

If driving helped your parent attend church, senior centers, clubs, visits, or errands, plan connection on purpose so transportation changes do not become isolation.

Errands and household tasks

List regular errands: bank, post office, hair appointments, laundry, pet needs, trash, and shopping. Decide what can be delivered, scheduled, delegated, or done during planned visits.

Emergency access

If your parent no longer drives, make sure they can still get help quickly. Review emergency contacts, spare key/access, nearby helpers, and local emergency plans.

Conversation help

Talk about safety without making your parent feel like a burden.

Driving conversations can feel emotional because they touch independence and identity. Start with care, specifics, and one next step.

  • “I know driving matters to you. I want to understand what feels hardest about changing it.”
  • “Can we talk about what happened and what would make things safer?”
  • “I am not trying to take away your life. I want to help protect it.”
  • “Let’s look at rides for appointments first so you are not stuck.”
  • “Can we ask your doctor or another qualified professional what they recommend?”

Before a family discussion

Try to gather specific examples, transportation alternatives, medical appointment needs, grocery/meal needs, and names of people who can help. A plan usually lands better than a demand.

Review services that can help

Transportation options

Possible ways to help your parent get around.

Community and public options

  • County senior transportation programs
  • Paratransit or reduced-fare transit
  • Senior center rides or shuttle programs
  • Volunteer driver programs
  • Veterans transportation resources, if eligible

Private and family options

  • Private transportation providers
  • Home care or companion care agencies that provide rides
  • Family ride schedule
  • Trusted neighbor or friend, with clear boundaries and permissions
  • Delivery services for groceries, pharmacy items, and household basics
Reminder: Contact each organization directly to confirm service area, eligibility, cost, advance scheduling, wheelchair or mobility-device support, caregiver ride-along rules, and whether rides cross county lines.

If your parent refuses

Resistance may be grief, fear, or loss of control.

Refusal does not mean the concern disappears. It may mean the conversation needs support from a doctor, occupational therapist, care manager, local aging agency, attorney, licensing agency, or another qualified professional depending on the situation.

  • Stay specific: describe the driving concern, not your parent’s character.
  • Offer alternatives before or alongside restrictions.
  • Ask what they are most afraid of losing.
  • Document concerns and who was contacted.
  • Escalate when immediate safety is at risk.
General information note: This guide is for organization and planning only. It is not medical, legal, financial, transportation-safety, licensing, or care-management advice. For decisions about driving safety, medical fitness, legal authority, insurance, licensing, medications, or urgent safety concerns, contact the appropriate qualified professional, agency, or emergency service.

One next step

Do not start with every transportation problem at once.

Choose the next trip your parent needs most: a doctor’s appointment, groceries, pharmacy, church, senior center, or one essential errand. Then search for one ride option and confirm how scheduling works.
