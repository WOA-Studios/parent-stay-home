Guide

Services That Can Help an Aging Parent Stay Home

A plain-language guide to common supports families may use when a parent wants to remain at home safely.

Search verified listings Open organizer tools

Start here

You may not need every service.

When a parent starts needing help, it can feel like everything is urgent at once. Start with the concern in front of you: food, rides, safety, paperwork, medical follow-up, or daily support. Then choose the service category that matches that concern.

  • Write down what changed and when you noticed it.
  • Choose the need that feels most urgent today.
  • Ask whether the concern can be helped with one service, one checklist, or one phone call.
  • Confirm current service area, eligibility, cost, and availability directly with each organization.
Urgent safety note: If your parent may be in immediate danger, may have fallen, may be having a medical emergency, may be unable to get to the phone, or the situation feels urgent, call 911 or the appropriate local emergency service right away.

Common supports

Service categories that may help at home.

These categories are a starting point. Availability, eligibility, cost, and coverage may vary by county, provider, program, and situation.

Senior transportation

Transportation may help when driving is no longer safe, appointments are being missed, groceries are difficult to get, or your parent needs rides to errands, senior centers, social events, or medical care.

Ask before scheduling: Does the ride need advance notice? Is wheelchair or mobility-device support available? Does the service cross county lines? May a caregiver ride along?

Meal delivery and food assistance

Meal support may help when shopping, cooking, standing for long periods, carrying groceries, or remembering meals becomes harder.

Ask before applying: Who is eligible? Are meals delivered hot or frozen? How often are meals delivered? Is there a cost, donation request, or income requirement?

Home safety and aging-in-place modifications

Home safety help may include grab bars, bathroom safety, ramps, railings, lighting, stair support, accessibility changes, and other modifications that may reduce risk at home.

Ask before hiring: Is the provider licensed or qualified for the work needed? Do they provide assessments? Are permits required? Is there financial assistance or a county program?

Senior move management

Move managers may help with downsizing, sorting belongings, planning a move, packing, unpacking, organizing, and coordinating transitions when staying in the current home may no longer fit the situation.

Ask before hiring: What services are included? Is there an hourly rate or package? Do they handle donations, disposal, floor plans, packing, or coordination with movers?

Local aging resources

County, state, and aging-network organizations can often help families understand services, benefits, caregiver support, nutrition programs, transportation, home safety programs, and local next steps.

Ask when you call: What programs serve my parent’s county? Are there eligibility rules? Is there a waiting list? Who should I call next if this office cannot help?

Care navigation and referral resources

Referral or navigation services may help families understand options, but they may not provide direct care themselves. Some may be commercial, referral-based, or paid by participating providers.

Ask before using: Is this service free to the family? How are referrals chosen? Are providers paying to participate? Does the service cover your parent’s location?

Match the need

Use the concern to choose where to start.

If appointments are being missed

Start with Senior Transportation. Also gather doctor, specialist, pharmacy, and insurance details in the Parent Emergency Information Sheet.

If meals or groceries are getting harder

Start with Meal Delivery & Food Assistance. Notice whether the issue is shopping, cooking, appetite, memory, mobility, or income.

If you are worried about falls or hazards

Start with the Home Safety Checklist, then review Home Safety & Aging-in-Place Modifications.

If you do not live nearby

Start with the Long-Distance Caregiving Checklist. Identify local contacts, medical communication access, key access, and a just-in-case plan if your parent cannot be reached.

If paperwork or accounts are unclear

Start with the Important Papers Checklist and the Financial Information Checklist.

If you are not sure what changed

Start with What to Do When Your Parent Starts Needing Help. It can help you name what is happening and choose one next step.

Before you call

Questions that reduce back-and-forth.

  • What county or service area do you cover?
  • Who is eligible?
  • Is there a cost, fee, donation request, insurance requirement, or income requirement?
  • Is there a waiting list?
  • What information should I have ready before applying or scheduling?
  • Who should I call if this service is not the right fit?
  • Are services available for someone with mobility, memory, language, or accessibility needs?

Keep notes as you call.

Write down the person you spoke with, the date, the next step, any eligibility requirements, and whether follow-up is needed. If several family members are helping, shared notes can prevent duplicate calls and missed information.

Trust and safety

Service listings are starting points, not endorsements.

Parent Stay Home provides informational listings and planning resources. We do not endorse providers or guarantee services, availability, cost, eligibility, quality, safety, or outcomes. Contact each organization directly to confirm current details before relying on the information.

General information note: This guide is for organization and planning only. It is not medical, legal, financial, or care-management advice. For decisions about healthcare, legal documents, benefits, taxes, accounts, medications, home safety, or urgent concerns, contact the appropriate qualified professional, agency, or emergency service.
