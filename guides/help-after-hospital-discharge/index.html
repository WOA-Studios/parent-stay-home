What to Do After a Parent Leaves the Hospital

A calm starting point for the first days and weeks after discharge.

Open emergency sheet Search verified listings

Start here

Hospital discharge can feel like too much at once.

A parent may leave the hospital with new medications, follow-up appointments, equipment needs, food concerns, transportation needs, and instructions that are hard to remember. Start by writing down what must happen in the next few days.

  • Read the discharge papers before leaving, if possible.
  • Ask who to call with questions after your parent gets home.
  • Confirm medication changes and follow-up appointments.
  • Check whether the home is safe for the parent’s current condition.
Urgent safety note: If your parent may be in immediate danger, may be having trouble breathing, chest pain, signs of stroke, severe confusion, a serious fall, uncontrolled bleeding, or another urgent medical concern, call 911 or the appropriate local emergency service right away.

Before leaving the hospital

Questions to ask before discharge.

Medication changes

  • What medications are new?
  • What medications should stop?
  • What dose and schedule should be followed?
  • Are there side effects or warning signs to watch for?
  • Which pharmacy should receive the prescriptions?

Follow-up care

  • Which doctor or specialist should be seen next?
  • When should the appointment happen?
  • Who should schedule it?
  • Are lab tests, imaging, therapy, or wound care needed?
  • Who should receive hospital records?

Home health or therapy

  • Was home health ordered?
  • Which agency will call?
  • When should the first visit happen?
  • What services are included: nursing, physical therapy, occupational therapy, speech therapy, or aide support?
  • Who should you call if the agency does not contact you?

Equipment and supplies

  • Is a walker, wheelchair, commode, shower chair, hospital bed, oxygen, or other equipment needed?
  • Who orders it?
  • Who delivers it?
  • Is it covered by insurance?
  • What should be done if it does not arrive?

First days at home

What may need attention right away.

Transportation

Your parent may need a ride home from the hospital and rides to follow-up appointments, therapy, lab work, or pharmacy visits.

Meals and food

After hospitalization, cooking or shopping may be harder than before. Ask whether meals, groceries, or temporary food support are needed.

Home safety

Look at stairs, rugs, lighting, bathroom access, bed height, pathways, and whether your parent can safely get to the bathroom, kitchen, phone, and entrance.

Medication safety

Do not rely on memory alone.

Hospital stays often change medication routines. Write down the current list and compare it with what your parent was taking before the hospital visit.

  • Use the exact medication name, dosage, frequency, and time of day.
  • Ask whether any old medications should be stopped or removed from the home.
  • Ask the pharmacist or prescribing doctor about interactions or duplicate medications.
  • Make sure the list is shared only with people your parent trusts or has authorized, when appropriate.
Medication safety note: Do not start, stop, change, combine, or skip medications without speaking with the prescribing doctor, pharmacist, or another qualified healthcare professional.

If you do not live nearby

Set up a local support plan.

The first days after discharge can be especially stressful when you are coordinating from another city or state. Decide who can physically check on your parent if you cannot get there.

Who can check in locally?

Identify a nearby relative, trusted neighbor, friend, faith-community contact, building manager, front desk, senior community office, or home care aide who may be able to help when appropriate.

Who is allowed to receive information?

Ask each doctor’s office, hospital system, pharmacy, therapy provider, and insurance plan what authorization forms may be needed so trusted helpers can receive information when appropriate.

Watch for changes

Know who to call if something does not seem right.

Before discharge, ask for written instructions about warning signs, what is expected, what is not expected, and who to call after hours.

  • Who should we call with medication questions?
  • Who should we call if symptoms return or worsen?
  • What signs mean we should seek urgent medical help?
  • Is there an after-hours number?
  • Should we call the primary care doctor, specialist, home health agency, hospital discharge line, or emergency services?

One next step

Start with the next 72 hours.

You do not have to solve everything today. Focus on what must happen first: medications, food, transportation, follow-up appointments, home safety, and who can help if something changes.
